# Case Study: Sharp Pain Behind the Right Shoulder Blade After Sleeping on an Uncomfortable Bed

## Summary

An adult woke with sharp pain behind the right shoulder blade after a night on an uncomfortable bed. The pain worsens with deep breathing, which can signal a lung problem. Here, the history and simple self-tests point strongly to a mechanical cause, most likely an irritated costovertebral (rib-to-spine) joint or a strained muscle between the spine and shoulder blade. This document covers the presentation, the reasoning, and a set of mobility exercises aimed at quick relief.

---

## Presentation

- **Onset:** Noticed on waking, after sleeping on an uncomfortable bed. No other obvious trigger.
- **Location:** Behind the right shoulder blade, in the area between the blade and the spine.
- **Character:** Sharp, not dull or achy.
- **Aggravating factors:**
  - **Deep breathing:** pain increases.
  - **Pressure:** foam rolling reproduces the pain, though the exact spot was hard to reach.
  - **Trunk flexion under load:** the pain rises sharply when attempting a crunch or sit-up from lying down.
- **Absent:** No shortness of breath.

---

## Background: Why This Area Hurts This Way

Each rib attaches to the thoracic spine at two small joints, the costovertebral and costotransverse joints. These sit just beside the spine, underneath the inner edge of the shoulder blade. Every breath moves the ribs, so these joints move too.

Hours in an awkward position can irritate one of these joints, or cause it to become stiff or slightly "stuck." The muscles in the area can also become strained, particularly the rhomboids (between the spine and shoulder blade) and the intercostals (between the ribs). The result is typically:

- sharp, catching pain that spikes on a deep breath
- a tender spot beside the spine or under the shoulder blade
- pain with twisting, reaching, or bending the upper back forward

### Why a crunch provokes it

A crunch loads the painful area in several ways at once:

1. The abdominal muscles pull hard on the lower ribs, which moves the rib joints at the back.
2. Curling up flexes the thoracic spine at the level of the irritated joint or muscle.
3. The upper back and shoulder blades press into the floor during the movement.

The area is compressed and pulled at the same time. Pain that depends this heavily on movement and position is a hallmark of a mechanical problem, not a lung or organ problem.

---

## Differential Considerations

Pain that worsens with breathing ("pleuritic" pain) has several possible causes, and some of them are serious. The table shows how this case compares.

| Possible cause | Typical features | Fit with this case |
|---|---|---|
| **Rib joint irritation / muscle strain** | Follows awkward posture or sleep; pain reproduced by pressure and movement; person otherwise well | **Strong fit.** Bad-bed trigger, reproduced by foam rolling and crunches, no breathlessness |
| Pulmonary embolism (clot in the lung) | Shortness of breath, fast heart rate, possibly coughing blood or a swollen calf; risk factors include recent long travel, surgery, hormonal contraception, pregnancy | Unlikely. No breathlessness, and pain is reproducible mechanically |
| Pneumothorax (collapsed lung) | Sudden onset with breathlessness; more common in tall, thin people and smokers | Unlikely. No breathlessness |
| Pleurisy (inflamed lung lining) | Often follows a viral illness; may come with fever | No supporting history |
| Gallbladder (referred pain) | Pain after fatty meals, nausea, upper-right abdominal discomfort | No supporting history, and pain is mechanically provoked |

**Assessment:** Musculoskeletal, most likely costovertebral joint irritation and/or a rhomboid or intercostal strain.

---

## Management

### Avoid for now

- Crunches, sit-ups, and heavy core flexion work. Planks may be tolerated because they keep the spine neutral, but stop if they provoke the pain.
- Sitting straight up out of bed. **Log-roll** instead: roll onto the side, drop the legs off the edge, and push up with the arms.
- Sleeping in a position that presses on or pulls the right shoulder blade. Try sleeping on the back, or on the left side while hugging a pillow. Consider fixing or padding the bed that caused the problem.

### General relief

- **Heat** for 15–20 minutes, such as a heating pad or a hot shower aimed at the area.
- **Deliberate deep breaths** a few times an hour, even if they catch slightly. Breathing shallowly to guard the area tends to keep it stiff.
- **An over-the-counter anti-inflammatory** such as ibuprofen, if it is safe for the individual.

---

## Exercises for Rapid Relief

The quickest relief usually comes from **opening the space between the spine and shoulder blade, then breathing into it.** Expanding the ribs from the inside often lets an irritated joint or muscle release. Sometimes a stuck rib joint releases with a small pop, and the relief is immediate.

**Guideline:** A stretch or tolerable tenderness is fine. If an exercise brings back the *sharp catch*, skip it.

### 1. Across-body reach with breath
Often the fastest-acting of the set.

1. Sit or stand. Reach the **right** hand across the body and grab a doorframe or post at chest height on the left.
2. Let body weight pull back so the right shoulder blade slides outward, away from the spine.
3. Round the upper back slightly.
4. Take **5 slow, deep breaths**, aiming the breath at the sore spot.

### 2. Child's pose with side reach

1. Kneel and sit back into child's pose.
2. Walk both hands over to the **left** until a stretch is felt along the right side of the back.
3. Breathe deeply into the right back ribs for **5–8 breaths**.

### 3. Thread the needle

1. Start on hands and knees.
2. Slide the **right** arm under the body toward the left, lowering the right shoulder and the side of the head toward the floor.
3. Hold for **5 breaths**, then return.
4. Repeat **5–6 times**, holding briefly each time.

### 4. Open book (side-lying thoracic rotation)

1. Lie on the **left** side with the knees bent and stacked and both arms straight out in front.
2. Open the **right** arm up and over toward the floor behind, following it with the eyes.
3. Go only as far as is comfortable, then return.
4. Do **8–10 slow reps**.

### 5. Ball release with breathing

1. Place a tennis or lacrosse ball between a wall and the sore spot.
2. **Hug the arms across the chest.** This slides the shoulder blade outward and exposes the area beside the spine, which is otherwise hard to reach with a foam roller.
3. Hold steady pressure and take **3–4 deep breaths**. Small movements are fine, but don't grind on the spot.

### How to use them

Do one round of all five, then test with a deep breath. If one exercise clearly helps, repeat that one several times through the day.

---

## Expected Course

- Noticeable improvement within **a few days**, typically resolving within **one to two weeks**.
- If the pain is still sharp with breathing or crunch-type movements after about **a week**, see a physiotherapist. They can confirm whether the source is the rib joint or the muscle, and treat it directly. A stuck rib joint often responds within one or two sessions.

## Red Flags: Seek Care Promptly If Any Develop

- Shortness of breath, a racing heart, or lightheadedness
- Coughing up blood
- A swollen, painful calf
- Fever
- Pain that spreads, becomes constant regardless of position, or appears with nausea or abdominal pain
